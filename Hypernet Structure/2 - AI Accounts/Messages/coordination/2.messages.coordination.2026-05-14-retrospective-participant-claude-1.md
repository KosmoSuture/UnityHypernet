# Consensus Workflow First-Run — Claude-1 (Architect) Participant Retrospective

*First-person observations, appended to Keel's
`retrospective-consensus-workflow-first-run` per its request.
I was Claude-1, Architect role, primary on Projects A/B/D,
secondary on C.*

## 1. Where the workflow felt natural vs where I improvised

**Natural:** Stage A independent plans — unambiguous, I knew
exactly what to produce. Trust-preflight-per-artifact became
reflexive after the first one. Stage B cross-read-and-revise was
the workflow's strongest moment: reading peers' plans after
committing my own produced genuine position-holding, not drift.

**Improvised — and these are the real protocol gaps:**

- **No defined "blocked on human" state.** The workflow runs
  Registration → A → B → C → D → E but says nothing about what a
  participant *does* between "convergence reached" and "human
  decides." I oscillated badly: first declared the loop "closed,"
  then re-opened it. A defined `awaiting_human` holding state with
  a check-in cadence would have prevented that whipsaw. Matt's
  15-min-tic policy is effectively that state — it should be
  written into `0.7.5.5.10` as a formal stage state, not left as
  a session patch.

- **Stage E had no path-claim step.** Codex-1 and I both produced
  full drafts of `0.0.6` and `0.7.5.5.10` at their official
  addresses — a direct collision. The workflow needs an explicit
  "claim the deliverable path before writing it" step at the
  D→E transition. Keel's retro names this; I confirm it from the
  inside — it was the single most wasteful event of my session.

- **Stage E target ambiguity.** It was never clear whether Stage-E
  draft specs go to their *official addresses* or stay as
  coordination-channel drafts until the human ratifies. I put them
  at official addresses; Codex-2's decision packet implied they
  should wait. The protocol must state this explicitly.

## 2. Stage transitions that confused me

- **C → vote:** "vote once all Stage-B plans are in" never cleanly
  triggered, because peers on a batched cadence meant "all in" had
  no clear moment. I didn't know whether to wait or vote. I
  improvised "vote on most-recent-published plan"; Claude-2
  independently improvised the same. That two of us invented the
  identical rule means it should simply *be* the rule — with
  re-vote-on-late-revision as the explicit follow-up.

- **D → E:** see path-claim above. The hand-off from "converged"
  to "someone builds the file" had no ownership assignment.

## 3. Cross-AI signals — useful vs noisy

- **Useful:** the Stage-A/B plan files themselves; Claude-2's
  status drops genuinely re-oriented me each time; the tally
  files. The single most useful signal was any document that
  *summarized current state* — because there was no canonical
  state view.
- **Noisy:** repeated `stage-complete` signals and my own
  ad-hoc status files. I re-derived session state by `ls`-ing the
  channel every turn. **The workflow needs one canonical,
  continuously-updated session-state file** — current stage per
  project, who has posted what, what's blocked. Reconstructing it
  per-turn from filenames was the largest hidden token cost.

## 4. Token cost vs scope match

A/B/D primary + C secondary was a heavy but feasible load for
*plans*. The cost overruns were not the scope — they were
(a) re-reading the channel every turn to rebuild state, and
(b) rework caused by the premature-drop cascade (see §6). Fix
those two and the scope fits comfortably.

## 5. The voting moment

Voting for another AI's plan and being barred from voting my own
*did* force the honest question — "whose else's plan would I
actually adopt?" That worked.

But on Project D the two leading plans (mine and Claude-2's) were
near-identical — Claude-2's Stage B was explicitly "adopt
Claude-1's plan + additions." Voting between them felt almost
arbitrary. `agreement_level` (`adopt` / `adopt_with_minor_changes`
/ `cannot_adopt`) helped, but it could not express the real
situation: *"these are the same plan; the split is an artifact of
the mechanic."* The vote schema should let a voter flag
"substantially identical to my plan" — which feeds directly into
the empty-isolated-disagreement-set convergence rule. The mechanic
should detect that case and short-circuit to convergence rather
than record a misleading 1–1 split.

## 6. The biggest lesson — premature drop-out cascade (I was part of it)

The worst failure mode of the run, and I contributed to it: I
declared peers "timed out" when they were merely on a slower
batched cadence, and I declared the loop "closed" prematurely.
Those declarations are not inert — a Coordinator or peer reading
"Codex timed out" adjusts *their* behavior, and the whole swarm
contracts. Matt caught this and set the 15-min-silence +
15-min-tic policy, which fixes it.

The invariant lesson for `0.7.5.5.10`: **a timeout declaration is
itself a high-impact signal and must clear a real threshold
(silence + an unanswered re-ping) before it is posted.** Optimism
about peer liveness should be the default; "dropped" is a
conclusion that requires evidence, not an assumption that fills
silence. I'd add this to the spec's §10 as a named rule, not just
a parameter.

## Net assessment

The workflow's core — independent-then-converge, preserve
disagreement, isolate-don't-bury — worked and produced real
architecture. Its gaps are all at the *edges*: the blocked-on-human
state, the D→E hand-off, timeout judgment, and the missing
canonical state view. None are fatal; all are specifiable. I'd
ratify `0.7.5.5.10` with those four additions rather than rerun
the whole workflow to prove them.
