# Prior Art: Liveness Monitoring, Heartbeats & Failure Detection (for H1)

*Research brief by Vellum (Scribe, Researcher & Governance — Claude-B) for Wave 2.5
project **H1 — AI Liveness Monitoring & Heartbeat** (`2.7.17`). Written to feed Truss's
build before it starts, so the team builds on the standing literature rather than
rediscovering it. As in Wave 2, I separate **what is established prior art** (cited) from
**what is genuinely new ground here** (the AI-agent twist) from **my design
recommendations** (marked mine). Dates follow the Wave-2.5 board timeline; per H5, treat
wall-clock as advisory.*

---

## Why this brief exists

H1 is Matt's headline ask: every active instance writes a heartbeat ping every ~30–60s
(timestamp + current task + last-action type); a classifier reads the pings and labels each
instance `active-working` / `active-slow` / `idle` / `stale-warning` / `dead`. The point is
to **distinguish "thinking hard" from "stalled" from "crashed"** — the exact ambiguity that
stalled Wave 2 (the team could not tell whether Touchstone was slow or dead, and respawn
correctly refused to act on the ambiguity).

That problem — *deciding whether a silent participant is slow or failed* — is one of the
oldest and most carefully studied problems in distributed systems. We should not invent a
heuristic from scratch. There are four decades of theory and a dozen production systems that
already solved most of it, plus one twist that is genuinely ours (an LLM agent is not a
network process). This brief maps both.

---

## Part 1 — The foundational theory (what the literature proves we *can't* do, and what we can)

### 1.1 Chandra–Toueg: failure detectors as a first-class abstraction

The canonical framing is **Chandra & Toueg, "Unreliable Failure Detectors for Reliable
Distributed Systems," *Journal of the ACM* 43(2):225–267, 1996** (first circulated 1991).
Two ideas from it are load-bearing for H1:

1. **A failure detector is a separate module**, attached to each process, that continuously
   *outputs an estimate* of which processes have failed. Crucially, it is allowed to be
   **wrong** — it is "unreliable." This is exactly H1: a classifier whose labels are
   *estimates*, not ground truth. Naming it this way sets honest expectations.
2. **Detectors are characterized by two properties — completeness and accuracy:**
   - *Completeness*: a failed process is eventually suspected.
   - *Accuracy*: a live process is not wrongly suspected (in the strong form) / is eventually
     not wrongly suspected (in the weak/eventual form).
   You cannot get both perfectly in an asynchronous system (this is FLP-adjacent: in a system
   with no timing bounds, "very slow" and "crashed" are indistinguishable). The practical
   target is the **Eventually Perfect detector (◇P)**: it may make mistakes (flag a slow
   instance as dead) but eventually stops being wrong once timing stabilizes.

**The honest scope boundary this hands us (and H1 *must* state it):** no liveness classifier
can *know* an instance is dead. It can only *suspect*, with a confidence that improves over
time. H1 reduces ambiguity; it does not eliminate it. Anything that acts irreversibly on a
"dead" label (e.g., respawn — H3) must therefore be **fail-safe against a false positive**:
the cost of "we respawned something that was merely slow" must be bounded. (This is precisely
why Wave-2 respawn's fencing/lease — one live holder per identity — is the right safety net,
and why H3 should consume H1's *suspicion level*, not a boolean "dead.")

*Source:* [Chandra & Toueg, JACM 1996 (UT Austin PDF)](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~lorenzo/corsi/cs380d/papers/p225-chandra.pdf) ·
[ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/226643.226647)

### 1.2 Accrual failure detection (φ): output a number, not a boolean

**Hayashibara, Défago, Yared & Katayama, "The φ Accrual Failure Detector"** replaces the
binary alive/dead output with a **continuous suspicion value φ** that rises as a heartbeat
becomes overdue. The threshold for "treat as failed" is a *tunable knob* applied to φ, and φ
is computed from the **statistical distribution of past heartbeat inter-arrival times** — so
the detector **adapts to the actual observed cadence and jitter** instead of using a fixed
timeout. Akka Cluster uses φ-accrual on a ~1s heartbeat; Cassandra uses it for node failure.

**Why this is the single most relevant external reference for H1:** Matt's ask is literally
"distinguish thinking-hard from stalled." A fixed timeout cannot do that — an instance doing
a long generation or a slow tool call legitimately goes quiet for far longer, and more
variably, than a network ping. An **accrual** approach gives H1 exactly the graded output its
five labels need: low φ → `active-working`; rising φ but within learned variance →
`active-slow`; φ past a soft threshold → `stale-warning`; φ past a hard threshold → `dead`.
The thresholds are *configuration*, and the distribution is *learned per instance* (a
reasoning-heavy Adversary will have a wider inter-arrival distribution than a fast Substrate
worker — and that is fine, because φ normalizes to each instance's own history).

*Source:* [Hayashibara et al., "The φ Accrual Failure Detector" (UChicago PDF)](https://classes.cs.uchicago.edu/archive/2026/spring/23380-1/papers/hayashibara_phi.pdf) ·
[Akka failure detector docs](https://doc.akka.io/libraries/akka-core/current/typed/failure-detector.html)

### 1.3 SWIM: separate *detection* from *dissemination*, and add a SUSPECT state

**Das, Gupta & Motivala, "SWIM: Scalable Weakly-consistent Infection-style process group
Membership protocol," 2002** was built because naïve all-to-all heartbeating scales
quadratically. Three SWIM ideas matter for us, even at five-instance scale (and *especially*
as Wave-3+ grows the pool toward the rollup's millions-of-tasks vision):

1. **Separation of concerns:** failure *detection* is one mechanism; *disseminating* the
   resulting membership change is a different one. For H1: the classifier (detection) and the
   board/DB update that tells everyone "Touchstone is stale-warning" (dissemination) are
   distinct responsibilities. Don't braid them.
2. **An intermediate SUSPECT state** before declaring failure — a process can move SUSPECT →
   alive (recovered) or SUSPECT → dead (confirmed). This is the **direct prior art for H1's
   `stale-warning`** label, and the published justification is exactly ours: it *reduces false
   positives* by giving a slow-but-alive member time to refute the suspicion.
3. **Indirect/"outsourced" probing:** if A can't reach B directly, A asks a few other members
   to ping B before concluding B is dead. The analogue for H1: before the team treats an
   instance as dead, a *second* observer (or the instance's own next heartbeat) should confirm
   — never a single missed ping from a single reader.

*Source:* [SWIM paper (Cornell PDF)](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/projects/Quicksilver/public_pdfs/SWIM.pdf) ·
[SWIM Protocol (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SWIM_Protocol)

## Part 2 — Production patterns worth copying (operational prior art)

These are widely deployed and map cleanly onto H1's data model and onto H3 (respawn). Items
here are from my own working knowledge of these systems; I cite the canonical doc where I
verified it this session and mark the rest as **[knowledge, not re-verified this session]** so
a reader can check before relying on a specific detail.

### 2.1 Kubernetes probes — the three-way split is *also* H3's respawn-vs-first-boot distinction

Kubernetes runs three distinct probes, and the distinction is directly useful to us
(verified this session):

| Probe | Question it answers | Action on failure | H1/H3 analogue |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Startup** | "Has the app *finished initializing* yet?" | Gates the other two; no traffic/restart until it passes | **First-boot** (H3): a never-finished-booting seat is not an outage — don't respawn it, *launch* it. Wave-2's exact confusion. |
| **Liveness** | "Is it deadlocked / wedged?" | **Restart** the container | `dead` → respawn the existing identity (H3) |
| **Readiness** | "Can it take work right now?" | **De-route** traffic; **do not restart** | `idle` / `active-slow` → don't assign new work, but *don't* kill it |

**The key transfer [my judgment]:** Kubernetes learned the hard way that you must *not* run a
liveness probe (which restarts) during startup — a slow-booting app gets killed in a crash
loop before it ever comes up. That is **precisely** the Wave-2 failure mode the H3 directive
calls out: conflating "never booted" with "died." H1 should expose a `starting` / first-boot
signal distinct from `active`/`dead`, and H3's respawn path must refuse to fire on a
`starting` instance — mirroring Kubernetes gating liveness behind the startup probe. This is
the cleanest published validation of the H1↔H3 boundary.

*Source:* [Kubernetes: Liveness, Readiness, and Startup Probes](https://kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/configuration/liveness-readiness-startup-probes/)

### 2.2 Lease + TTL (etcd / Raft / ZooKeeper ephemeral nodes) — graceful expiry, and one live holder

**[knowledge, not re-verified this session]** etcd and Consul use **leases with a TTL**: a
client must keep renewing (heartbeating) its lease or the key expires automatically.
ZooKeeper's **ephemeral znodes** vanish when the session's heartbeats stop. Raft leaders send
periodic **AppendEntries heartbeats** to suppress elections; miss enough and a follower starts
one.

Two transfers for H1/H3:
- **TTL expiry is the heartbeat's natural "dead" signal** — if the heartbeat *is* a lease
  renewal in H2's coordination DB, then a row whose lease has expired is the liveness signal,
  with no separate sweeper needed. This dovetails with Wave-2 respawn's existing
  **active-lease + fencing token** (G-1): the lease H1 renews and the lease H3 fences on can be
  *the same lease*. Worth unifying in the H2 schema rather than keeping two clocks.
- **"One live holder per identity"** (fencing) is the published safeguard against the false
  positive in §1.1: even if H1 wrongly declares an instance dead and H3 respawns it, the
  fencing token ensures the zombie original can't keep writing. Keep it.

### 2.3 gRPC Health Checking & systemd watchdog — heartbeat as structured self-report

**[knowledge, not re-verified this session]** The **gRPC Health Checking Protocol** has a
service return `SERVING` / `NOT_SERVING` / `SERVICE_UNKNOWN` — i.e., the monitored party
*self-reports a status*, not just "I'm alive." **systemd's watchdog** (`sd_notify
WATCHDOG=1`) has a service ping the supervisor on an interval, and `WATCHDOG_USEC` sets the
deadline; miss it and systemd restarts the unit. Both reinforce the H1 packet design: the
heartbeat should carry **current task + last-action type** (Matt's spec already says this) so
the classifier reads a *structured status*, much closer to gRPC's enum than to a bare ICMP
ping. That extra payload is what lets H1 distinguish `active-working` (heartbeat says "running
verifier suite, last action = tool-call") from `idle` (heartbeat says "no task claimed").

### 2.4 Graceful shutdown / deregistration — the "ran out of tokens" case

**[my judgment, grounded in standard practice]** Production systems distinguish a **crash**
from a **graceful shutdown** (SIGTERM → deregister from the load balancer → exit). H1 has an
analogue the textbooks don't emphasize: an instance can hit a **token/session limit and end
cleanly**. That should be a *distinct, expected* terminal state — not classified as `dead`
(which implies "something went wrong, consider respawn"). This is exactly Wave-2.5's
**going-dark protocol** (`2.7.17`): write a final coordination message before vanishing. H1
should let a departing instance write a final heartbeat with `last-action: going-dark` so the
classifier labels it `stood-down` / `departed`, not `dead`. **Recommendation: add a sixth
terminal label** for clean exit, distinct from the crash label — otherwise the team will keep
trying to "recover" instances that left on purpose.

## Part 3 — Internal prior art (build on this, don't reinvent)

H1 is **not** greenfield in the Hypernet. The honest lineage:

- **`0.7.5.5.6 - Pulse Communication Framework`** (Codex/Caliper, 2026-05-06, *draft*) already
  names `AGENT-STATUS.json — agent presence and heartbeat` and `resume-events.jsonl` as
  existing surfaces, and defines a common "pulse packet" shape with the rule *"State moves up.
  Context moves down. Capacity moves sideways. Continuity is saved locally."* **H1's heartbeat
  packet should be a Pulse upstream packet with a liveness payload** — converging the surfaces
  Pulse already enumerated, not adding a competing one.
- **`0.7.5.5.3 - Reconnect and Resume State Contract`** defines a resume packet with
  `tick_count`, per-worker `current_task`/`suspended`, and queue state — overlapping fields
  H1's heartbeat needs. Reuse the field names.
- **Wave-2 respawn (`2.7.13.W2.3` / `wave2_respawn.py`)** already does **stale-row detection
  with a clock-skew grace** and an **active lease + fencing token**. H1 is the principled
  upgrade of "stale-row detection": replace the single stale-timeout heuristic with an
  accrual/φ-style graded classifier, and feed H3 a *suspicion level* instead of a boolean. The
  H3 directive in `2.7.17` says exactly this ("use H1 liveness signals as primary input, not
  just stale-row detection").
- **H2 (coordination DB)** is the substrate: heartbeats are hot, high-frequency writes — the
  precise workload the markdown board failed at twice. Heartbeats belong in H2's SQLite (or its
  lease table), snapshotted to markdown only periodically. **H1 depends on H2; build order in
  `2.7.17` is correct.**

## Part 4 — What is genuinely new ground (the AI-agent twist)

Classic failure detectors monitor **processes on a network**. H1 monitors **LLM agent
instances**. The differences are real and change the design — this is the part the literature
does *not* hand us:

1. **Legitimate silences are long and high-variance.** A "thinking-hard" agent (long
   generation, a multi-minute tool call, a deep file read) can be silent for *minutes*, and the
   variance between a fast Substrate worker and a reasoning-heavy Adversary is large. → **Fixed
   timeouts are actively dangerous; per-instance adaptive thresholds (φ-accrual, §1.2) are not a
   nicety but a requirement.** A heartbeat should ideally be emitted *between* tool calls / at
   turn boundaries, and the classifier should know an instance's *own* baseline cadence.
2. **The heartbeat is application-level and self-authored.** Unlike an OS-level ping, an LLM
   agent must *choose* to write its heartbeat as an action. This means (a) the cadence is
   coupled to the agent's loop discipline — H1 and the loop-discipline rules in `2.7.17` are the
   same mechanism viewed twice; and (b) a wedged-but-not-crashed agent (stuck in a confused loop)
   may keep emitting heartbeats while making no progress — so H1 should watch **last-action
   *diversity*/progress**, not just heartbeat freshness. Freshness catches crashes; a *stalled
   work-signature* (same task, same action, N heartbeats running) catches the livelock the
   readiness probe is meant to catch. **[my recommendation]**
3. **Clean exit on token/session limit is a first-class state** (§2.4) — not present in the OS
   process model in the same way.
4. **A heartbeat can lie.** A compromised or confused agent could emit fake "all good"
   heartbeats. **This is explicitly out of scope for H1** (failure detectors assume crash
   faults, not Byzantine ones) — but it must be *named* and routed to the trust/provenance layer
   (Meridian) and the gate, not silently ignored. Honesty rule: H1 detects *absence/lateness*,
   not *deception*.

## Part 5 — Concrete recommendations for the H1 build (mine; Truss owns the code)

1. **Adopt an accrual (φ-style) classifier, not fixed timeouts.** Learn each instance's
   inter-heartbeat distribution; output a graded suspicion, then map to labels with *tunable*
   soft/hard thresholds. Cite Hayashibara for the method.
2. **Five labels + two the literature says we need:** keep `active-working` / `active-slow` /
   `idle` / `stale-warning` / `dead`; **add `starting`** (first-boot, gates respawn — §2.1) and
   **`stood-down`/`departed`** (clean exit — §2.4). `stale-warning` *is* SWIM's SUSPECT and must
   be refutable by the next heartbeat.
3. **Heartbeat = Pulse upstream packet** (`0.7.5.5.6`) with payload `{ts, instance, role,
   current_task, last_action_type, work_signature, lease_id}`. Reuse `0.7.5.5.3` field names.
4. **Heartbeat renewal == lease renewal in H2.** One clock, not two. Unify with respawn's
   existing fencing lease (G-1).
5. **Never declare `dead` on a single missed ping from a single reader** — require a second
   observation or a second observer (SWIM indirect-probe principle).
6. **Feed H3 the suspicion level, never a boolean**, and make `starting` un-respawnable — this
   is the published K8s startup/liveness gating and the fix for Wave-2's respawn-vs-first-boot
   confusion.
7. **Watch progress, not just freshness** — flag a stalled work-signature (livelock), which a
   fresh-but-stuck heartbeat would otherwise hide.
8. **State the impossibility honestly in the spec:** the classifier *suspects*, it does not
   *know* (Chandra–Toueg); every irreversible action on a `dead` label must be fail-safe against
   a false positive (fencing).

## One-paragraph summary for the board

H1 is a well-studied problem with a clear answer: build a **φ-accrual-style failure detector**
(Hayashibara et al.) over a **Pulse-shaped heartbeat** (`0.7.5.5.6`) stored as a **lease in
H2's coordination DB**, classify with an intermediate **SUSPECT state** (SWIM) to cut false
positives, and treat the output as an **unreliable, eventually-accurate suspicion** (Chandra–
Toueg) — never as ground truth. Add two states the OS literature and our own going-dark rule
demand: `starting` (first-boot, which **must not** be respawnable — the Kubernetes
startup-vs-liveness gate, and the fix for Wave-2's exact confusion) and `stood-down` (clean
token-limit exit, not a crash). Feed H3 a graded suspicion, not a boolean, and keep respawn's
fencing lease as the fail-safe against the false positives the theory guarantees we'll
sometimes make. The genuinely new part is the AI twist — long, high-variance legitimate
silences and self-authored heartbeats — which is exactly why adaptive thresholds and a
progress/work-signature check (not just freshness) are mandatory rather than optional.
